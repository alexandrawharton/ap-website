📚 Core Topics & Skills

Rhetorical Situation

Every piece of writing has a context. Identify the author's purpose (to inform, persuade, entertain), their audience (who they're writing for and what that audience values), the genre and occasion, and how those factors shape every choice the writer makes.

Claims, Evidence & Reasoning

Strong arguments have a clear claim (what you're arguing), relevant and specific evidence (facts, examples, quotes), and reasoning that explicitly connects the evidence back to the claim. The reasoning is what most students skip — don't skip it.

Rhetorical Appeals

Ethos (credibility — the author establishes trust), Logos (logic — statistics, facts, logical structure), and Pathos (emotion — word choice, anecdotes, imagery that creates an emotional response). Be able to identify all three in any passage and explain WHY the author chose each appeal.

Style & Tone

How an author writes is as important as what they say. Analyze diction (word choice), syntax (sentence structure), tone (the author's attitude toward the subject), and figurative language. Ask: why did the author choose this word or this sentence structure here?

Synthesis Writing

The synthesis essay gives you 6–7 sources on one topic. Your job is to form your own argument and use at least 3 sources as evidence to support it. Do NOT just summarize the sources — use them to prove your point. Cite them as (Source A), (Source B), etc.

Rhetorical Analysis Essay

You'll be given a speech, essay, or article and asked to analyze how the author uses rhetorical choices to achieve their purpose. Write a thesis that identifies specific strategies, then analyze WHY each choice works — don't just label it (avoid "they use pathos here").

Argument Essay

You argue a position on an open-ended prompt using your own knowledge and examples. Strong argument essays have a nuanced thesis, specific real-world evidence, at least one counterargument addressed and refuted, and a line of reasoning that ties everything together.

🔑 Must-Know Vocabulary

EthosAppeal to credibility — establishing the author as trustworthy or qualified
LogosAppeal to logic — using facts, data, statistics, and reasoning
PathosAppeal to emotion — word choice, imagery, or stories that create feeling
ThesisA specific, arguable claim that responds to the prompt with a line of reasoning
DictionThe specific words an author chooses and the effect those choices create
SyntaxSentence structure — length, punctuation, fragments, or parallelism for effect
ToneThe author's attitude toward the subject (e.g., urgent, ironic, reverent)
CounterargumentAn opposing view that a writer acknowledges and then refutes or concedes
AnaphoraRepeating the same word or phrase at the start of successive sentences for emphasis
JuxtapositionPlacing contrasting ideas side by side to highlight their differences

🎯 How Essays Are Scored (1–6 per essay)

RowWhat Graders Look For
Thesis (0–1 pt)Makes a specific, defensible claim. Does NOT just restate the prompt. Earns 1 point or 0 — no partial credit.
Evidence & Commentary (0–4 pts)Uses relevant, specific evidence AND explains how it supports the thesis. More explanation = more points.
Sophistication (0–1 pt)Demonstrates a nuanced understanding — complex line of reasoning, effective use of rhetorical choices, or compelling style. Hard to earn; don't chase it.

💡 Study Tips

  • Write a timed essay every week. AP Lang is a writing exam — the only way to improve is to write under real conditions. Set a 40-minute timer, write the full essay, then compare to a rubric. Repeat.
  • Never just label a rhetorical device — always explain the effect. "The author uses anaphora" earns nothing. "The author's anaphora creates a sense of urgency, pressuring the reader to act immediately" earns points.
  • Your thesis is everything. A vague thesis caps your score at a 3. A strong thesis names the author's strategies AND explains how they work together to achieve the purpose. Write your thesis first, then build the essay around it.
  • Read one op-ed, editorial, or speech every day. The more non-fiction you read actively — identifying claims, evidence, and appeals as you go — the faster and more naturally you'll analyze passages on the exam.
  • For the synthesis essay, pick a side quickly. You have limited time. Spend 5 minutes reading sources, 5 minutes outlining, and 30 minutes writing. Don't try to be neutral — strong arguments take a clear position.
