📚 The 9 APUSH Periods

Period 1 (1491–1607): Contact & Exploration

Indigenous societies before contact, European exploration motivations, the Columbian Exchange (diseases, crops, animals), and early Spanish, French, and English colonial goals.

Period 2 (1607–1754): Colonial America

English colonial regions (New England, Middle, Southern), colonial economies, the development of slavery, and tensions with Indigenous peoples. Salutary neglect and colonial self-governance.

Period 3 (1754–1800): Revolution & New Nation

French and Indian War, colonial grievances, the Revolution, Articles of Confederation, Constitution debates (Federalists vs. Anti-Federalists), and the first party system.

Period 4 (1800–1848): Democracy & Expansion

Market revolution, Jacksonian democracy, westward expansion (Manifest Destiny), Missouri Compromise, early abolitionism, and the Second Great Awakening's social reform movements.

Period 5 (1844–1877): Civil War Era

Sectional tensions, causes of the Civil War, Lincoln's wartime policies, the 13th/14th/15th Amendments, Reconstruction's successes and failures, and the Compromise of 1877.

Period 6 (1865–1898): Gilded Age

Industrialization, robber barons vs. labor unions, the Populist movement, the New South, and the closing of the frontier. Immigration patterns and social Darwinism debates.

Period 7 (1890–1945): Imperialism to WWII

American imperialism, the Progressive Era (muckrakers, women's suffrage, trust-busting), WWI and isolationism, the Great Depression and New Deal, and WWII including the home front.

Period 8 (1945–1980): Cold War America

Origins of the Cold War, Korean and Vietnam Wars, the Civil Rights Movement (strategies and legislation), Great Society programs, and the social movements of the 1960s–70s.

Period 9 (1980–present): Contemporary America

Reagan Revolution and conservatism, end of the Cold War, globalization, 9/11 and the War on Terror, economic shifts, and ongoing social and political debates.

✍️ Essay Skills That Win Points

ThesisA historically defensible claim that responds to the prompt with a line of reasoning
ContextualizationExplain the broader historical context BEFORE the time period of the prompt
EvidenceUse specific historical examples beyond just referencing the documents (DBQ)
Document AnalysisExplain the document's HAPPy: Historical situation, Audience, Purpose, Point of view
Sourcing (DBQ)For 3+ docs: explain why source, purpose, or historical situation is relevant
ComplexityExplain nuance — corroboration, tension, or cause/effect across time periods

💡 Study Tips

  • Learn the rubric by heart. Every point on the DBQ and LEQ has a specific rubric. Know exactly what "contextualization" and "complexity" require — they're the hardest points to earn without deliberate practice.
  • Practice writing timed essays. A DBQ gives you 60 minutes (including 15 min reading time). Write under real conditions so pacing becomes automatic before exam day.
  • Don't memorize dates — understand causation. APUSH tests WHY things happened, not WHEN. Ask yourself: what caused this? What was the result? How does it connect to the next period?
  • Make a one-page timeline for each period. Write 5–6 key events per period in your own words. This forces you to synthesize rather than just re-read your notes.
  • The multiple choice is stimulus-based. Every question comes with a document, image, or graph. Practice reading primary sources quickly and identifying the author's argument or historical context.
